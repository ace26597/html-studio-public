Electric or petrol? What a new car really costs over five years in the US

Short answer: for a typical driver buying a new SUV today, an electric car and a comparable petrol car cost about the same over five years, with the EV usually a little dearer. For sedans and pickups the EV is clearly more expensive, mainly because of resale value. The winner depends on three things more than on the car itself: how much the EV loses in resale value, how many miles you drive, and whether you can charge at home.

The EV saves real money on running costs. AAA's 2026 study puts charging 66–70% cheaper than gasoline, and maintenance is lower too. Those savings are mostly cancelled by faster depreciation, higher insurance, finance charges and fees. In AAA's 2026 figures the EV ends up $15,400 dearer over five years for a medium sedan and $3,940 for a pickup, $1,160 dearer in a compact SUV, but $495 cheaper in a medium SUV.

The federal clean-vehicle tax credit is gone. The IRS says it is not allowed for any vehicle acquired after 30 September 2025. A few states still pay. Massachusetts pays $3,500, and California's first-time-buyer program pays up to $3,500. A rebate that size is enough to tip a close comparison to the EV.

The five-year total: AAA's 2026 side-by-side

AAA's annual Your Driving Costs study is the most complete like-for-like comparison published this year. It prices top-selling 2026 models in four categories, as electric, gas and hybrid versions. It assumes 15,000 miles a year and averages depreciation over five years. It also assumed gas at $4.15 a gallon and 18 cents per kWh. Multiplying its yearly figures by five gives the five-year totals below.

The EV came out more expensive in three of four categories. The gap is large for medium sedans: $15,400 over five years, almost all from depreciation. It is small for compact SUVs, at $1,160. In medium SUVs the EV is $495 cheaper. In every category the hybrid was cheapest. AAA's own reading: EV charging savings "were often outweighed by higher depreciation, fees and finance costs".

What AAA's method means for you: "license, registration, taxes" includes sales tax, which follows the purchase price. "Finance charges" assume a loan. A cash buyer pays no finance charges, which narrows the gap a little in the EV's favour. AAA does not say whether it deducted any incentive. It uses sticker prices, so state rebates are probably not included.

What swings it. In this example, depreciation adds about $8,000 to the EV's cost. Energy and maintenance take about $7,900 off it. So small changes in your situation decide the result:

  • Miles driven. Every extra mile widens the EV's running-cost lead. At these prices the EV breaks even at about 18,000 miles a year.
  • Charging at home only. Fast chargers cost about three times as much per kWh as home power. If you never use them, the gap at 15,000 miles shrinks to roughly zero.
  • Resale value. If the EV held its value like the average car (41.8% loss), it would be about $4,200 cheaper than the petrol Equinox over five years.
  • A state rebate. Any point-of-sale rebate larger than about $1,560 turns the result in the EV's favour (see Incentives).

Purchase price and resale value

EVs still cost more to buy, but the gap has narrowed. Kelley Blue Book puts the average price paid for a new EV in October 2026 at $56,220. The average for all new vehicles was $51,109. In September, Cox put the EV premium over the rest of the market (which it calls "ICE+") at $5,611, against a 2025 average of $8,665. These averages do not include consumer incentives, and EVs get bigger ones than average: 11.3% of the price, against 6.8% across all vehicles.

Resale value is the EV's biggest cost. iSeeCars studied five-year-old cars sold between March 2025 and February 2026. EVs had lost 57.2% of their value, against 41.8% for the average vehicle and 35.4% for hybrids. The cars in this study were bought around 2021. The large EV price cuts since then pushed down what used EVs are worth. A car bought today at a lower price may lose less, but nobody can measure that yet.

The used market is catching up. In September the average used EV was listed at $36,767, only 6.2% above non-EVs.

Fuel vs electricity

Charging at home costs well under half as much per mile as gasoline. Fast charging does not. Gasoline averaged $3.995 a gallon in EIA's survey for the week of 9 November 2026. AAA showed $3.87 on 13 November. EIA expects $3.89 for 2026 as a whole and $3.73 in 2027. Home electricity averages 18.2¢ per kWh in 2026. State prices range widely: in August 2026 they ran from 12.72¢ in Nevada to 29.75¢ in California and 47.18¢ in Hawaii. Public DC fast charging averaged $0.540 per kWh in Q3 2026.

The EPA's fueleconomy.gov figures give similar results for other pairs. The 2026 Fuel Economy Guide behind them assumes 15,000 miles, $4.00 gasoline and $0.15/kWh electricity. That electricity price is below today's average, so the labels flatter EVs slightly.

How much you charge at home matters. The Department of Energy expects that 64% of charging will happen at single-family homes and 20% at fast chargers by 2030. A driver without a home charger who relies on fast chargers will pay more per mile than for gasoline.

Maintenance, repairs, tyres and the battery

EVs are cheaper to maintain, but by how much depends on who is counting. An EV has no engine oil, spark plugs or timing belt. The Department of Energy and Argonne estimate scheduled maintenance at 6.1¢ a mile for an EV against 10.1¢ for a petrol car. Consumer Reports surveyed owners and found EV drivers "saving an average of 50% on maintenance and repair" over the car's life. AAA's figures show a smaller gap. Its maintenance cost includes an extended warranty, wear-and-tear repairs and a set of tyres, as defined in its 2025 edition. For a compact SUV AAA has $1,402 a year for the EV and $1,954 for petrol.

  • Tyres wear faster. EVs are heavier and have more torque. Michelin says EV tyres can wear up to 20% faster. Argonne estimates that a 30% shorter tyre life would raise EV maintenance by about 7%, from $0.060 to $0.064 per mile.
  • Crash repairs cost more. Mitchell's Q2 2026 figures put the average repairable collision claim at $5,684 for EVs against $4,955 for petrol cars. This is mostly paid through insurance and shows up in premiums.
  • Battery failures are rare. Recurrent tracks more than 30,000 EVs. Outside recalls, under 4% of batteries have been replaced, and 0.3% for 2022-and-newer models. Fleet data from Geotab show batteries losing 2.3% of capacity a year. Many makers offer 8-year/100,000-mile battery warranties. That covers a typical five-year ownership. Out of warranty, a replacement typically costs $5,000 to $16,000.

Insurance and registration fees

EVs usually cost more to insure, though estimates vary a lot. Insurify's quote data put the average EV at $3,159 a year, against $2,218 for petrol cars. That average includes older cars. For newer vehicles the gap is 18%, about $501 a year. ValuePenguin's Illinois data found EVs from brands that also make petrol cars cost the same to insure as petrol cars. Teslas and Rivians cost much more. AAA 2026 found the EV dearer to insure for medium sedans ($2,070 vs $1,704) but cheaper for medium SUVs ($2,070 vs $2,423). Most states add an EV fee. At least 42 states charge an extra yearly registration fee for EVs, to make up for the gas tax EV drivers don't pay. NCSL lists fees from $75 in Minnesota to $242 in Indiana and, in New Jersey, $270 for the year to June 2027, rising to $290 in 2028. Texas lists "$400 registration for a new EV. $200 annual registration fee for EVs." Over five years that comes to roughly $1,000–$1,200, depending on how many years the first $400 covers (the saved text does not say). A federal fee is also on the table. The BUILD America 250 Act would charge $130 a year. As of 13 November it had not been approved by the full House or Senate.

Incentives available today

The federal credits are gone; a handful of states still pay. According to the IRS, the new clean vehicle credit "will not be allowed for any vehicle acquired after September 30, 2025", and the same applies to the used-EV and commercial (leasing) credits. The home-charger credit ended for equipment placed in service after June 30, 2026. Earlier studies that showed big EV savings often counted the old credit, worth up to $7,500. That money no longer exists for a car bought today.

The new federal car-loan interest deduction does not favour either kind of car. It lets buyers deduct up to $10,000 a year of interest on a loan for a new car, petrol or electric. The test is whether the car "has undergone final assembly in the United States".

EVs carry bigger incentives. In October 2026 the average EV incentive was 11.3% of the price, against 6.8% for all new vehicles. Negotiate.

What a rebate does to the worked example. The Equinox EV was about $1,560 dearer over five years. With Massachusetts' $3,500 it comes out about $1,940 cheaper. New York's $2,000 leaves the EV about $440 ahead. In Colorado, the Equinox EV's price is above $35,000, so it gets only the $750 base credit in 2026, which leaves the EV about $810 behind. Bought in 2027, when the extra $2,500 extends to cars under $40,000, the Equinox EV would likely get $4,500. That would put it about $2,940 ahead. (The example's $200 registration fee is the Texas rate; use the calculator for your own state's fee.)

What could not be confirmed, and the limits of this estimate

  • Depreciation for cars bought today. The EV loss of 57.2% comes from 2020–2021 cars, which lost value partly because new-EV prices were cut later. No five-year figure exists for the Equinox EV, Ioniq 5 or other current models. This is the biggest uncertainty in any five-year comparison.
  • Matched insurance quotes. No source gives both the Equinox EV and the petrol Equinox under one method. The example uses AAA's compact-SUV averages instead.
  • Destination fees. The saved Car and Driver pages don't say whether starting prices include them. The maker sites blocked the fetcher.
  • Tucson price. The 2027 Hyundai Tucson price is Car and Driver's estimate, not official.
  • Home-charging share. The 20% fast-charging share is a DOE projection for 2030, not a measurement of today's drivers.
  • Battery warranty rule. We could not confirm whether the EPA rule requiring an 8-year/80,000-mile battery warranty from 2027 models survives the EPA's reconsideration.
  • KBB and Edmunds cost-to-own figures could not be retrieved.
