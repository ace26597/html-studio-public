Should we swap our gas furnace for a heat pump?

A sourced report · researched 13 November 2026 · prices and programmes change, so check the dates next to each figure

Short answer. Today's cold-climate heat pumps still heat at Minneapolis winter temperatures. At 0–5 °F, field-measured heat-pump-only efficiency (not counting defrost or backup heat) is roughly twice that of electric resistance heat. Even so, CEE, which implements Minnesota's Air Source Heat Pump Collaborative, found in its field study that these units need backup heat below about −15 °F. A full swap from gas to an all-electric heat pump will cost a Minneapolis household more to install. CEE's modelling also found it costs more to run than gas, at every gas price it tested.

The setup the evidence supports best is a hybrid ("dual-fuel") system: a heat pump that takes over cooling and most of the heating, with the gas furnace kept for the coldest hours, billed on Xcel's discounted electric space-heating rate. It is most worth doing when your central AC needs replacing anyway; the state collaborative says "there is no reason to buy an air conditioner any longer in Xcel Energy territory". Expect running costs to come out roughly even with gas, or modestly lower. Xcel's billing data show about $75–$224 a year lower, results the authors call tentative.

The federal tax credit is gone for 2026 installations. Minnesota's income-qualified rebate programme has not launched. Utility rebates that require installation by 31 December 2026 are the main help available right now.

How this is worked out, and what it leaves out

Gas used = heating part of the bill ÷ $1.04 per therm, the 2025 Minnesota average [s5]. Heat delivered = therms × 100,000 Btu × 95%, assuming a modern furnace; an older furnace delivers less heat for the same bill, so this would overstate the heat pump's cost. Heat pump electricity = heat ÷ (COP × 3,412 Btu per kWh) [s66], multiplied by the price you chose.

This is a rough, all-electric estimate. It does not model a hybrid system, where the furnace covers the coldest hours. Fixed monthly charges are not counted. If you disconnected gas entirely you would also save CenterPoint's $9.50 monthly basic charge. Air-conditioning costs are left out too, and they would change little if the heat pump replaced your AC. For a real figure, use your actual therms from your bills and a contractor's model for your house.

1. How well cold-climate heat pumps really perform below freezing

Finding: they keep working at Minneapolis temperatures, but efficiency roughly halves between mild and very cold weather, and field results run below lab ratings. COP is units of heat out per unit of electricity in. A COP of 1.0 is a plain electric heater; a COP of 2 delivers twice the heat for the same electricity.

What "cold-climate" means on paper

  • The NEEP cold-climate specification, which utilities use for rebates, requires a COP of at least 1.75 at 5 °F [s1]. Its listed COPs leave out defrost energy: "Do not include the power required for defrost cycling". Real-world numbers will therefore be lower.
  • ENERGY STAR Cold Climate adds that heating capacity at 5 °F must be at least 70% of the 47 °F capacity [s26].
  • The US Department of Energy's Cold Climate Heat Pump Challenge pushed manufacturers further. In the lab, all prototypes kept COP above 2.1 or 2.4 at 5 °F and delivered at least 100% of nominal capacity at 5 °F [s7].

What field measurements show

  • DOE Challenge field test, 22 sites (US homes plus Canadian homes and lab houses), 2022–24. Excluding backup heat and defrost, the median COP at 0–5 °F was 1.9, below the lab result. Backup resistance heat supplied a median of about 11% of heat at 5 °F. In the coldest bins, −10 to 0 °F, backup elements were on 25% of the time [s7]. Those winters were mild, with only eight of the 22 sites logging 10 or more hours below 0 °F. The data on truly cold hours is thin.
  • Natural Resources Canada tested Challenge units in Ottawa, Quebec and Manitoba. The best units achieved a COP of 1.8 at −26 °C (about −15 °F), counting all operating modes [s50].
  • Minnesota: CEE field study, 2015–17, older-generation units. Annual system COP averaged 1.9 for all-electric systems and 1.3 for dual-fuel systems, whose figure includes the propane furnace. A small ductless unit kept running down to −25 °F, but at approximately 20% of its rated 5 °F capacity. CEE concluded that "these units should not be used without backup at extreme" temperatures, which it puts below −15 °F [s30].
  • Northeast (Cadmus/NEEP, 43 metered homes). Measured seasonal COP was 2.39, which is 72% of the rated value. Ducted whole-home systems managed only 2.09, 60% of rated [s46].
  • NREL, 12 ducted homes in Washington, Montana and Colorado. Compressor-only seasonal COP averaged 2.5 (range 1.7–3.5). At two sites, backup heat cut the overall system COP by more than 30% [s41].

Capacity in deep cold

Efficiency is only half the story. The other half is whether the unit can produce enough heat on a −15 °F night. One current product that grew out of the DOE Challenge [s7], a Bosch 4-ton unit, is listed for operation down to −13 °F. Its catalogue shows output falling from 35.0 thousand Btu/h at 47 °F to 27.8 at −10 °F, about 79% of its mild-weather output [s59]. Below −13 °F it is outside its rated range, so on a −15 °F night the backup heat carries the load. That is one product, not a market survey.

Sizing matters as much as the unit itself. In CEE's Minnesota study, one home's actual heating load was 31% below its calculated design load [s30]. For an all-electric system, NREL found that sizing for cooling rather than heating means leaning on resistance backup, which turns a Minnesota saving into a loss (see the running-cost section). Comfort held up in the DOE field test, where average heating satisfaction rose from 3.61 to 3.88 out of 5. Morning setbacks, though, often triggered resistance heat at 30-35 °F [s7]. The DOE field report points toward steady setpoints or gradual setpoint changes instead.

2. What it costs to install

Finding: a ducted cold-climate heat pump typically costs more than replacing a furnace and central AC. National research puts the gap at several thousand to over ten thousand dollars. Minnesota quotes from contractor websites come in lower but are unverified.

  • The 2024 NREL/LBNL national study by Wilson et al. covers cold-climate homes that already have ducts and central AC. It found a mean $24,000 for a high-efficiency cold-climate heat pump, against $11,000 for a new furnace and AC. Figures are in 2022 dollars. Panel upgrades are not modelled separately: the study says they are "implicitly included in an unknown portion of the cost data" [s3].
  • A 2024 Northeast cost study (NESCAUM) found that fossil systems plus AC averaged $10,000–$13,000, against $12,000–$23,000 for heat pumps. That study did not require cold-climate models [s27].
  • Rewiring America's national estimate for a 1,500–2,500 sq ft home is $17,000–$23,000 (median $19,500). The organisation adds that colder climates tend to fall at the high end [s55].
  • The EnergySage marketplace's 2026 average quote is $14,529 for a ducted heat pump and $14,353 for a hybrid [s54]. These are quotes, not paid prices. EnergySage notes that most of its quotes come from Massachusetts.
  • A Twin Cities contractor's 2026 guide says a ducted cold-climate system costs $10,200 to $19,500 installed. A dual-fuel add-on that keeps the existing furnace runs $6,500 to $16,000, and quotes vary 20-30% for the same job [s9]. This is marketing material that cites other websites.

Extras that often come with a heat pump

  • Ductwork. Adding or replacing undersized ducts added about $4,500 in material in the Northeast study [s27]. NREL estimates larger ductwork at $7,000 [s3].
  • Electrical. Panel and circuit upgrades, when needed, added an average of $2,400 [s27]. Rewiring America says a 100-amp panel can "usually" handle electrification without an upgrade [s57]. Ask for a load calculation before accepting a panel upgrade.
  • Insulation first. NREL found that air sealing and insulation shrink the heat pump needed, but the envelope work itself cost a mean $9,000 [s3].

3. What it costs to run, compared with gas

Finding: in Minnesota gas is cheap and electricity is not, so an all-electric heat pump has to be very efficient to beat a modern gas furnace. Xcel's electric space-heating rate changes the picture a lot, and hybrid systems come out roughly even or slightly ahead.

The price gap

Minnesota homes paid an average of 15.82¢ per kWh for electricity in 2025 (preliminary) [s65]. They paid $10.98 per thousand cubic feet for gas [s5]. Minnesota gas carries 1,055 Btu per cubic foot [s14], which makes it about $1.04 per therm. Per Btu of energy bought, before any equipment efficiency, Minnesota electricity costs about 4.45 times as much as gas. Nationally the ratio is about 3.42. Minnesota gas is cheaper than the US average ($15.34 per thousand cubic feet), which is why national "heat pumps save money" headlines don't carry over directly.

Xcel's electric space-heating rate. Since fall 2025, Xcel customers who heat mainly with electricity pay a lower winter price. Citizens Utility Board gives air-source heat pumps that heat at least 50% of the home as an example. The state collaborative confirms it covers "a hybrid system consisting of an ASHP paired with a natural gas or propane furnace", as long as the heat pump is the primary heat source. The base energy charge is 6.537¢/kWh from October to May [s23]. CEE estimates the all-in winter price, with riders and fuel, at $0.11137 per kWh (about 11.1¢), against $0.16011 on the standard rate [s24]. The discount covers the whole home's electricity use, and the rate is described as 42% savings against the standard winter rate [s25]. You have to call Xcel to enrol.

The MN Air Source Heat Pump Collaborative's summary agrees: "hybrid-heat systems offer the lowest operational costs … while all-electric systems may increase bills for natural gas customers" [s52]. Emissions. Both main studies found a cut in emissions: CEE's paper with current emission factors, Wilson et al. across projected 2022–2038 grids. The CEE paper says added electric emissions "in all cases … are more than offset by the reductions in gas-related emissions" [s24]. 5. When a heat pump does not make sense

  • You want to remove gas entirely and cut your bills. The CEE model shows an all-electric system costing more to run than gas even on the heating rate: 28% more at $0.82/therm, and nine to 45% more at $1.06/therm, close to the 2025 Minnesota average [s24]. It can still be the right choice for emissions reasons, but not for money.
  • Your furnace and AC are both fairly new. The economics lean on replacing equipment that is due anyway. NREL's cost gaps assume an $11,000 furnace-plus-AC replacement you would otherwise buy [s3].
  • You won't or can't get Xcel's heating rate (or your utility has no equivalent). Without it, the break-even COP is around 4, well above the field-measured seasonal figures.
  • An all-electric system sized for cooling, or ducts that are too small. With resistance backup, sizing for cooling turned NREL's Minnesota median from a saving into a $96/yr increase [s42]. Undersized ducts push you into the duct work above ($4,500–$7,000).
  • A leaky, uninsulated house. Loads are high and backup heat runs more. Insulation first is cheaper per unit of comfort and earns Xcel's $600 bonus.
  • You plan to run a nightly setback. The field tests show recovery from setbacks triggers resistance heat [s7]. If you won't change the habit, savings shrink.
  • You need a rebate to make it work and it's late in the year. Most 2026 utility rebates require installation by 31 December, and the state programme has not opened.

Where it clearly does make sense: homes now heated with propane, oil or electric baseboard; a gas home whose AC has died, where a cold-climate heat pump replaces the AC and the furnace stays as backup; and owners who will accept a modest annual cost premium for lower emissions.

6. How to decide: a short checklist

7. What could not be confirmed

  • Minnesota state rebate status. Every Minnesota Commerce Department page returned a bot check. "Not launched" rests on a secondary site checked in July 2026, plus search snippets dated October 2026 that we could not save. How Minnesota will apply DOE's May 2026 rule change, and whether a gas home adding a heat pump will qualify, is unknown.
  • Rebate amounts disagree. CEE's Minneapolis table lists utility rebates for a ducted cold-climate unit as $1,100 gas / $900 electric, while Xcel's own sheet says $1,500/$2,000. Programme caps also differ: $20,000 on CEE's page against $14,000 on the city's page. We rely on Xcel's own sheet for Xcel amounts.
  • 2027 rebates. The utilities' 2027–29 plans were not decided when this was written.
  • Minnesota installed costs. No primary Minnesota cost data was found; the Minnesota figures come from contractor and lead-generation websites.
  • Very low temperatures. No product-level COP figures at −13 to −15 °F from the NEEP list, and no numeric DOE Challenge results at −15 °F. The only Minnesota field study of ducted systems is from 2015–17 and used older equipment.
  • Exact Xcel all-in price. The $0.11137/kWh figure is CEE's estimate; Xcel publishes base charges, not all-in prices. CenterPoint's monthly gas cost for winter 2026–27 was not found.
  • A household bill estimate for your home. We did not invent one: it needs your actual gas use, house size and quotes.

8. How this was researched

Four research threads ran in parallel: cold-weather performance, installed cost, running cost and incentives. Together they saved and read 66 source documents. About 50 were usable; the rest were bot-check pages, landing pages or wrong documents. The cited sources are listed below. Primary sources were used where possible: DOE and national laboratory reports, NEEP, ENERGY STAR, the IRS, EIA, utility rate books and rebate sheets. Contractor and lead-generation sites were used only for Minnesota cost ranges and are labelled. Not covered: geothermal heat pumps, NEEA, Vermont, Maine, Michigan and Alaska field studies, RMI analyses, and Rewiring America's incentive calculator. Every marked figure was checked automatically against the saved source text, and the page was then reviewed independently.
